\chapter{Conclusion}\label{chap:Conclusion} \markright {\thechapter~Conclusion}

This chapter summarises the main findings of the comparison and derives recommendations for the investigated floor systems. The conclusions start with a review of the sustainability assessment criteria and provide application ranges of the residential and office case studies.

\section{Sustainability assessment criteria}
For the sustainability assessment, GWP and construction cost are treated as primary criteria to account for the ecological and economic dimensions. The comparison shows that the relationship between \(GWP\) and construction cost tends to align within systems of similar materialisation. Within one material family, lower material demand generally improves both environmental and economic performance. The conflict between GWP and construction cost becomes more pronounced when systems with different materialisation and construction processes are compared.

This is particularly visible in the residential case. Timber systems show lower \(GWP\), but higher modelled construction cost, whereas concrete systems show lower construction cost, but higher \(GWP\). This conflict is partly influenced by unfavourable model assumptions for timber, since timber costs are defined per cubic metre and implicitly include fabrication and connection effort. These cost components are likely not fully dependent on the material volume in practice. Under the present assumptions, timber is therefore mainly balanced for small spans, while concrete becomes more favourable for larger spans. TCC systems provide a relevant compromise across the investigated residential span range, as they combine parts of the environmental advantage of timber with the structural efficiency of concrete.

Nevertheless, the observed difference between concrete and timber systems remains an important research question. A carbon price would likely have stronger leverage on concrete systems than on timber systems and could therefore shift the balance between GWP and construction cost. This conflict should therefore be addressed by excluding clearly dominated systems first and then selecting among the remaining alternatives according to project-specific priorities. Pairwise comparison, sensitivity analyses and optional shadow carbon costs can make this trade-off explicit and prevent the final recommendation from being based on either GWP or cost alone.

In the office case, the conflict between GWP and construction cost is mainly observed at shorter spans. It can be attributed to the increased complexity of post-tensioning solutions and ribbed formwork. However, the results show that this additional complexity is already compensated for spans above \(8\,\mathrm{m}\). This strongly supports the use of post-tensioned slabs in the investigated office span range. Ribbed slabs become particularly relevant above approximately \(11\,\mathrm{m}\), where their reduced material demand increasingly compensates for their more complex geometry.

Mass is considered a secondary criterion for residential buildings, since acoustic floor build-ups reduce the differences in total system weight. For office buildings, mass is more relevant because the total weight is governed more directly by the structural cross-section.

Construction time is important, but project-dependent. It may become a primary criterion when prefabrication, short construction periods or reduced propping times are central to the project strategy.

\section{Slab systems for residential buildings}
No system performs best across all criteria and spans. The pairwise comparison shows that rectangular timber is favourable at very short spans, especially at \(3\,\mathrm{m}\), because of its low structural GWP and mass. However, it loses robustness as span increases because cost and construction height rise strongly. Flat TCC provides the best overall pairwise balance around \(5\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(8\,\mathrm{m}\), where it combines competitive GWP with moderate depth and short construction time. Rectangular concrete is strongest around \(6\) to \(7\,\mathrm{m}\), where its low cost and low construction height compensate for its higher GWP.

At longer residential spans, the pairwise comparison favours ribbed TCC at approximately \(10\,\mathrm{m}\), although post-tensioned concrete and flat TCC remain close alternatives depending on the weighting of cost, height and construction time. Ribbed TCC achieves very low structural GWP but requires substantially greater construction depth. Post-tensioned concrete provides a favourable low-cost and low-height alternative, while flat TCC offers a compromise between these alternatives through moderate depth and short construction time.

\subsection{Slab systems for office buildings}
The office comparison shows that no system performs best across all criteria and spans. Rectangular reinforced concrete is not recommended for the investigated span range. It is neither economically competitive nor functionally advantageous, as its material demand, construction height, and \(GWP_{struct}\) increase strongly with span.

Post-tensioned concrete provides the best overall balance in the pairwise comparison. It combines low construction height with competitive \(GWP_{struct}\), short construction time and competitive cost. The banded tendon layout achieves the highest total pairwise score across the full investigated span range and is therefore recommended as the robust default office solution. It benefits from tendons concentrated in the support strips and leaves the field regions largely free for further material-saving measures, such as formwork inlays. The distributed tendon layout is recommended only when a banded layout is not possible and only up to approximately \(12\,\mathrm{m}\).

Ribbed concrete is not the best overall office system in the pairwise comparison, but it is the strongest criterion-specific solution for \(GWP_{tot}\) and mass across the investigated spans. From approximately \(12\,\mathrm{m}\), it also becomes the most robust cost option. It is therefore recommended when minimising GWP or mass is prioritised and the larger construction depth is acceptable. If floor height or construction time are governing, the banded post-tensioned slab remains preferable.
