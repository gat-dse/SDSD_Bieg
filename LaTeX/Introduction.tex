\chapter{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}\markright{\thechapter~Introduction}

The construction sector faces substantial challenges arising from the growing global demand for floor area \cite{UNEP2025}. In emerging countries, this demand is largely driven by population growth and urbanisation. In developed countries, the challenge is usually shaped by an ageing building stock and the need for renewal, refurbishment or replacement. In Switzerland, approximately 60\% of buildings were constructed before 1960 and approach their end of their service life, making the challenge highly relevant today \cite{BFS2024_Bauperiode}.

At the same time, building construction remains a decisive contributor to global greenhouse gas emissions. In 2023, the construction of buildings alone was responsible for approximately 8\% of global energy- and process-related emissions \cite{UNEP2025}. Within the effort to limit climate change, the construction sector is therefore required to reduce its environmental impact substantially. In Switzerland, SIA~390/1 defines reduction pathways and calls for an 80\% reduction in greenhouse gas emissions during the construction of buildings until 2050 \cite{SIA390_1_2025}.

This creates a fundamental tension for the construction sector: The demand for floor area continues to grow, while the emissions associated with its provision must be reduced drastically. At the same time, this challenge provides an opportunity to further develop design strategies and construction processes in order to reduce the ecological footprint of the sector.

For structural engineering, this challenge has often been addressed by comparing materials primarily in terms of their specific greenhouse gas emissions \cite{Kaufmann2024_WhitePaper}. However, the environmental performance of a structure depends not only on the product-specific Global Warming Potential (GWP) of the materials used, but also on their mechanical performance and on the structural possibilities they enable. In addition, design choices can have indirect effects at the building level. In simple terms, a low-carbon material does not automatically lead to a low-carbon structure if larger quantities, greater structural depths, less efficient static systems or additional construction layers are required \cite{Kaufmann2024_WhitePaper}. Consequently, sustainability assessment in structural design must move beyond simple material comparisons and consider the interaction between product-specific material data, material-specific structural possibilities, functional requirements, and building-level implications.

During conceptual design, the potential to reduce embodied carbon is greatest, as the main decisions regarding structural system, material choice, span, and construction method have not yet been fixed \cite{broyles2024equations}. However, the large number of possible design scenarios makes comprehensive sustainability assessments difficult within the available time and financial resources. In engineering practice, early-stage structural assessments are often limited to simplified checks of ultimate limit state and deflection behaviour. They rarely account for additional requirements, such as vibration performance, fire resistance, and acoustic insulation, although these are often decisive for members with functional requirements \cite{Ammann2024}. Conversely, existing research on early-stage sustainability assessment is often based on empirical data and is not directly linked to structural engineering calculations \cite{broyles2024equations}.

There exists a clear need for tools that can systematically evaluate a wide range of design parameter combinations while satisfying the relevant design requirements at the early design stage \cite{Kaufmann2024_WhitePaper}. For a holistic comparison, such tools should account for ultimate limit state, serviceability limit state, vibration, fire resistance and building physics criteria. In addition, they should allow producer-specific material data, material performance, structural efficiency, cost, construction time and their variations to be considered consistently. This is necessary because sustainability comprises ecological, economic, and social dimensions and cannot be assessed solely based on GWP. 

Within buildings, floor systems represent a particularly relevant field of investigation. This comes, as slabs account for more than 50\% of the total concrete mass in conventional concrete buildings\cite{DBV_Heft50_2024}. In addition, slab are structural members that do serve in particular as functional parts. Therefore, the complete floor system, consisting of the structural cross-section and the floor build-up, form major levers for optimisation.

The objective of this thesis is thereby to assess the influence of different floor systems on the sustainability performance of a multi-storey residential or office building. For this purpose, an existing Python framework is further developed and applied to compare selected floor systems systematically. The considered systems are optimised with respect to sustainability in terms of GWP while fulfilling all required design criteria.

Although novel, structurally optimised, and digitally fabricated concrete floor systems have been developed in recent years \cite{Wangler2019, Menna2020}, the focus of this thesis is placed on floor systems that are already market ready. Hence, the comparison concentrates on floor systems that are frequently applied in Swiss construction practice.

This thesis is embedded in a broader research project conducted by Professor Walter Kaufmann’s research group at the Institute of Structural Engineering at ETH Zurich. The project examines sustainability in structural design independently of the materials used. It was launched in collaboration with industry partners and aims to identify the most important levers available to structural engineers in a practice-oriented manner and to quantify their effects using scientific methods. Within this project, the Python framework that was further developed within this thesis was initially created. In future, the knowledge generated within the project will be transferred to both prospective and practising engineers through block courses and continuing education formats \cite{kfmResearch2026}.
